## Key Facts

### Cownose Ray (Rhinoptera bonasus) — Seasonal Spine

- Cownose rays enter the Chesapeake Bay in May and are present through October. Arrival is Bay-wide by early May; peak activity in the lower and middle Bay tributaries runs May through July.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/cownose-ray
- Female rays, many of them pregnant, arrive first. They actively seek shallow tributaries to give birth, where relative safety from large oceanic sharks allows pups to mature.
  - Source: Smithsonian Environmental Research Center (SERC) — https://serc.si.edu/tracking-cownose-rays-improve-understanding-behavior-and-migration
- Birth timing: pups are born in mid-June, roughly 11 months after conception. Each female delivers one pup, approximately 12 inches wide and fully formed at birth.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above); also SERC blog — https://sercblog.si.edu/what-cues-cownose-rays-to-migrate/
- Mating occurs in June or July. Male rays depart the Bay after mating; females remain through October.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above)
- Schools can be very large — documented in the thousands and occasionally the tens of thousands in the broader Atlantic migration.
  - Source: SERC / Smithsonian Institution press release — https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/first-time-biologists-track-cownose-rays-florida-and-back
- Migration driver: northward arrival at the Bay is most strongly associated with day-of-year cues (endogenous timing) for males; water temperature (SST) drives female timing. Both sexes arrive by early May.
  - Source: Bangley et al. 2021, Ecosphere / Wiley — https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ecs2.3743
- Natal homing confirmed: rays return to the same estuaries where they were born to give birth and mate, meaning Hull Creek's population is likely self-sustaining across generations.
  - Source: VIMS 2018 — https://www.vims.edu/newsandevents/topstories/2018/cownose_ray_natality.php
- Will-supplied timing note: Will records their appearance as "early May" on the sightings list. This is consistent with the documented Chesapeake Bay arrival window. **The writer should use Will's firsthand "early May" framing; do not upgrade to a precise date.**
- Ecosystem signal: cownose rays feed on bivalves and invertebrates in soft-bottom shallows. Their presence in Hull Creek indicates adequate prey populations and appropriate water conditions (salinity, temperature, clarity).
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above)

### River Otter (Lontra canadensis) — Winter Rarity

- North American river otters are confirmed throughout the Chesapeake Bay watershed, including tidal creeks, marshes, and estuaries in Virginia. They are found along the tidal Potomac and its Northern Neck tributaries.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/river-otter
  - Source: Virginia DWR species page — https://dwr.virginia.gov/wildlife/information/northern-river-otter/
  - Source: USFWS species profile — https://www.fws.gov/species/north-american-river-otter-lontra-canadensis
- River otters are active year-round. Sightings from shore are more likely in late winter through early spring (January–April) when vegetation is sparse and otters travel more visibly along waterways. They are also more active during daylight hours in colder months.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above); Virginia DWR (above)
- Will's sighting (winter, from shore) is entirely consistent with documented behavior. This is a genuinely rare observation — otters are present in the watershed but are secretive; a clear shore sighting is uncommon.
- The subspecies in Virginia is Lontra canadensis laxatina.
  - Source: Virginia DWR (above)
- Ecosystem signal: river otters require clean water with healthy fish and invertebrate populations. Their presence is a strong indicator of water quality in Hull Creek.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Foundation — https://www.cbf.org/nature/otters/

### Lined Seahorse (Hippocampus erectus) — Rare / Crab Pot

- The lined seahorse is the correct species for this sighting. It is the only seahorse species native to the Chesapeake Bay.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/lined-seahorse
- Primary range in the Bay: the middle and lower Chesapeake, with the documented northern limit at approximately Calvert County, Maryland. In saltier years, documented as far north as the Bay Bridge.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above)
- VIMS monthly trawl surveys (running since 1955) cover the Rappahannock, York, and James rivers up to approximately 40 miles from each river mouth. VIMS typically finds 40–100 seahorses per year across this survey area.
  - Source: House & Home Magazine / VIMS reference — http://thehouseandhomemagazine.com/culture/seahorses-of-the-chesapeake-bay/
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above)
- RANGE CAVEAT FOR WRITER: Hull Creek drains to the Potomac River, which is north of the Rappahannock. The documented primary range is middle-to-lower Bay. A seahorse coming up in a Hull Creek crab pot is therefore at or beyond the northern edge of the documented range — which makes it a genuinely extraordinary sighting, exactly as Will characterizes it.
- **Do not state Hull Creek is within the seahorse's normal range. The sighting is verified as Will's firsthand observation; the biology makes it a rarity. Frame it as such.** UNVERIFIED whether this individual was a stray or representative of a seasonal salinity-driven northward push. The writer should not claim this is a typical Hull Creek occurrence.
- Breeding in the Bay occurs May–October. Seahorses cling to crab pots, pilings, and ropes — so the crab-pot capture is biologically plausible (not a fabricated scenario).
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above)
- Ecosystem signal: lined seahorses require underwater grass beds (SAV) for habitat. Their presence in the lower/middle Bay is tied to seagrass health, itself a water-quality indicator.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide (above); Elizabeth River Project — https://elizabethriver.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Lined-Seahorse-Web.pdf

### Sea Turtle — Rare / Washed Into Creek

- Two species are plausible for a Hull Creek sighting: loggerhead (Caretta caretta) and Kemp's ridley (Lepidochelys kempii). Both are documented in Virginia's Chesapeake Bay waters May–November.
- Loggerhead: uses the Chesapeake as an important summer foraging area; primarily juveniles (ages 5–15). Estimated 7,000–10,000 individuals use the Bay each summer. They prefer the deeper central mainstem and the mouths of tidal rivers; documented as rarely swimming more than a few kilometers upstream.
  - Source: VIMS — https://www.vims.edu/research/units/legacy/sea_turtle/va_sea_turtles/loggerhead.php
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/loggerhead-turtle
  - Source: Virginia DWR — https://dwr.virginia.gov/wildlife/information/loggerhead-sea-turtle/
- Kemp's ridley: the Chesapeake Bay is the single most important developmental habitat for immature Kemp's ridley turtles in the world. Unlike loggerheads, they prefer shallower, nearshore habitat; frequently found in tidal estuaries, seagrass beds, and shoal areas close to shore. More likely than loggerhead to enter a shallow tidal creek such as Hull Creek.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/kemps-ridley-sea-turtle
  - Source: Virginia DWR — https://dwr.virginia.gov/wildlife/information/kemps-ridley-sea-turtle/
  - Source: Virginia Herpetological Society — https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/reptiles/turtles/kemps-ridley-sea-turtle/index.php
- Both species: enter the Bay when water temperature exceeds approximately 18°C (May) and depart by November when it cools.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide for both species (above links)
- **Writer guidance on species:** Will's record does not name the species. The Writer should not assert which species it was. Appropriate framing: "a sea turtle washed into the creek — almost certainly a juvenile, either a loggerhead or a Kemp's ridley, both of which use these waters each summer." If Will can confirm size or shell shape, that would help (see Open Questions). Kemp's ridley is the stronger candidate given the shallow creek habitat.
- Both species are federally threatened. The fact that they appear in this system at all is ecologically meaningful.
  - Source: NOAA Fisheries — https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/kemps-ridley-turtle

### Swans — Rare / Occasional

- Two swan species occur in the Chesapeake Bay region: tundra swan (Cygnus columbianus) and mute swan (Cygnus olor).
- Tundra swan: a migratory visitor. Arrives in the Bay region in late October and early November; departs in early spring. Winters in shallow fresh and brackish waters of the Chesapeake and its tidal rivers. Identified by black bill and straight neck.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/tundra-swan
- Mute swan: year-round resident in the Bay region. An introduced species (escaped from a Talbot County estate in 1962; population has spread). Identified by orange bill and gracefully curved neck.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/mute-swan

### Belted Kingfisher (Megaceryle alcyon) — Occasional

- Confirmed as a common Chesapeake Bay species; resident and widespread breeder in the Virginia portion of the watershed. Nests in earthen banks along creeks and rivers. Fishes from overhanging branches over clear, shallow water — precisely the habitat Hull Creek offers.
  - Source: Chesapeake Bay Program field guide — https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/belted-kingfisher
  - Source: Alliance for the Chesapeake Bay — https://www.allianceforthebay.org/2024/05/diving-into-the-world-of-the-belted-kingfisher/
- Will's sighting list notes "seen on the water once." The Virginia Breeding Bird Atlas confirms kingfisher breeding in the Northern Neck region.
  - Source: eBird Virginia Breeding Bird Atlas — https://ebird.org/atlasva/species/belkin1/L4304332
- Kingfisher on the dock or creek edge is a plausible, seasonally available sighting any time May–October; some overwinter in Virginia.
- Ecosystem signal: kingfishers require clear water (visibility for diving) and healthy small-fish populations. Presence indicates good water clarity in Hull Creek.
