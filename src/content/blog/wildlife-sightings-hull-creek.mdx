The first sign that summer's actually arrived on Hull Creek isn't the heat. It's
the rays. Some morning in early May you're on the dock with your coffee and you
notice something moving differently in the water, not a fish, not a shadow,
and then you see the wingtips just beneath the surface, slow and deliberate, and
the whole flat shape of it gliding through. {/* TODO: Will to confirm — firsthand description of the visual moment from the dock: shadow passing, wingtips breaking surface, how many at once, how close */}

Cownose rays enter the Chesapeake in early May. The females come first, many of
them pregnant, seeking out shallow tributaries like this one to give birth.
By mid-June the pups arrive, roughly a foot across and fully formed, already
moving with that same unhurried wingbeat. Schools can run into the thousands in
the broader migration. What we see here is a small piece of something enormous,
and it's one of those moments that stops a conversation mid-sentence.

That's what Hull Creek does if you stay still long enough.

## The ones that stop you cold

The dock birds: ospreys working the shallows, a great blue heron standing in
the shallows like a piece of furniture, the occasional bald eagle on the thermals
above the treeline. They become familiar by the second morning. A good familiar.
But the Creek keeps a few surprises in reserve, and they tend to arrive on the
days you weren't looking.

One summer {/* TODO: Will to confirm approximate year */} we pulled the crab pot
at the dock and found a lined seahorse inside. A lined seahorse is the only
seahorse species native to the Chesapeake, and its documented range runs through
the middle and lower Bay, well south of here. Hull Creek drains to the Potomac,
which puts it at the edge of that range at best. Finding one in a crab pot at
this latitude is, by any measure, extraordinary. It went back in the water. We
still talk about it.

The sea turtle came a different year: a small one, washed up into the creek,
either a loggerhead or a Kemp's ridley, both of which pass through these Bay waters each
summer, and the Kemp's ridley in particular likes the shallow tidal habitat that
describes Hull Creek exactly. {/* TODO: Will to confirm size, appearance, condition of the turtle */} Both species are federally threatened. The fact that one
ended up in this creek at all feels like a privilege.

And once, in winter, from the shore: an otter. {/* TODO: Will to confirm sighting detail — elongated, low-slung, sleek brown, in or near the water? Confirm the ID is solid. */} North American river otters are
documented throughout the Chesapeake watershed, right down into the tidal
Virginia tributaries, but a clear sighting from shore is genuinely rare. They're
secretive. Winter strips the vegetation back and pulls the otters into daylight a
little more than usual, and that appears to be what happened. We counted
ourselves lucky.

## What the field edge offers

The Creek isn't the whole story. The cornfield that runs along the back of the
property has its own rhythm, and one afternoon a fox appeared at the edge of it,
unhurried, doing whatever foxes do at the boundary between cover and open

## Why this place holds what it holds

None of this is accidental. The <a href={withBase("/area")}>Northern Neck</a>
retains the kind of shoreline habitat, water quality, and human quietude that
most of the Mid-Atlantic coast has given away. Ospreys nest here in numbers that
researchers actually use as a proxy for Chesapeake Bay health. River otters
require clean water with robust fish and invertebrate populations. Seahorses
follow the seagrass. Sea turtles follow the forage. When all of them show up in
the same small tidal creek, it's saying something about the place.

We've put up the kayaks, stocked the <a href={withBase("/activities")}>dock with
the gear</a>, and kept the crab pot at the back porch for anyone who wants to
pull it in the morning. We can't promise you a seahorse. But we can promise
you the dock, the water, and the kind of attention that a few slow days on Hull
Creek tends to produce. That's where the good sightings come from.
