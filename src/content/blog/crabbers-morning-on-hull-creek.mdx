The alarm goes off at five and the cottage is still dark. This is not the kind of waking that requires convincing — you remember, the moment your feet hit the floor, that there are crab pots on the dock. Everything else can wait.

Hull Creek is flat at that hour. The water and the sky are the same gray-blue, both of them held in the twenty minutes before real light arrives. Osprey have already started working somewhere upstream — you can hear them before you see them, that sharp descending cry carrying across the still surface. {/* TODO: Will to confirm — osprey audible from the dock before dawn in summer, or does this soften to "by full light"? The dock planks are wet with dew. You don't need a flashlight, but you move carefully.

## The Pull

The cottage keeps crab pots at the dock, and the work of pulling them is slower and more satisfying than it sounds. You haul the line hand over hand, set the trap on the planks, and look at what came up. Some mornings it's a full house. Some mornings you count four crabs and feel unreasonably pleased with yourself. The size of the catch matters less than the fact of standing on a dock in Northumberland County at first light, doing what watermen on this part of the <a href={withBase("/area")}>Northern Neck</a> have done since before the pot was invented here.

What goes back into the water: anything undersized, and any immature females — you can tell them by the narrow, unformed apron on the underside of the shell. Virginia recreational rules are simple and easy to read before your stay at [mrc.virginia.gov](https://mrc.virginia.gov/regulations/VA-recreational-crabbing-rules.shtm); two pots per person require no license. Check current rules — they can change year to year.

By the time you've worked through the pots, the light has shifted. The fog that sits on the water in summer burns off in layers. A heron moves through the shallows with the patience of someone who has nowhere better to be. {/* TODO: Will to confirm — herons on the creek in summer mornings? ## The Morning After the Morning

This is when you go inside and make coffee.

The screened porch faces the creek. Sitting there with a mug while the rest of the house is still asleep is its own reward — it doesn't matter much what's in the basket. The point of a morning like this is that it happens before the day technically starts, and so you get two mornings: the quiet one on the dock, and then the ordinary one that follows.

The steam and Old Bay come later, when the shells go red and the smell of it fills the porch and announces breakfast in a way nothing else quite does. But that's a separate thing from the pull itself. The pull is its own hour, before the world joins in.
