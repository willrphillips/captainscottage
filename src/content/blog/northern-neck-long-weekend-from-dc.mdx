You leave DC on a Thursday afternoon and the city releases you slowly. South on 95, then the long swing east on Route 3 through King George County and into the Northern Neck — no tunnel, just the wide Potomac crossing on the Harry W. Nice Bridge and then Virginia spreading out flat and green on the other side. The drive is about two and three-quarter hours, and if you decide early that the weekend has already started, the whole thing goes faster than you'd think.

## Day One: Thursday

Check-in is four o'clock. On a clear late-summer evening that gives you nearly four hours of light before dark. Enough time to claim a bedroom, find a cold drink, and walk the dock before you've done anything else.

<a href={withBase("/the-cottage")}>The cottage</a> faces west, and the creek in front of it faces west too. The gold starts early — maybe six-thirty — and by seven-fifteen it's the kind of light you'll try to photograph and fail to capture. You won't mind.

After the sun goes down, the sauna is the move. The cedar barrel sits outside with a window toward the water. An hour in there followed by a drop from the dock into the creek is the quickest way to feel genuinely unwound. The contrast — the heat, then the cold, the water still warm from summer — resets something. Then the hot tub on the deck while the sky goes dark, and dinner whenever you get around to it.

## Day Two: Friday

By five-thirty the creek is flat and the ospreys are already working upstream. You set the crab pot the night before, and first light is the right moment to check it. Haul the line up hand over hand, lean out over the dock to see what came up. A good pull on this part of the creek has a way of waking a whole group up faster than coffee. That comes after, on the screened porch over the water.

Then, when the morning opens up, the kayaks. The creek runs shallow and brackish and it changes as the morning goes on — flat and glassy at dawn, working and bright by ten. Paddle upstream until you feel like turning around, then drift back.

The afternoon is easy. The water, a book, a hammock. By five o'clock, start the country low boil. A big pot of water, Old Bay, corn, potatoes, sausage, whatever the crab pot added. The smell of it fills the screened porch. This is the meal that most weekends here end up remembering — not because it's complicated, but because it isn't.

## Day Three: Saturday

Saturday afternoon belongs to <a href={withBase("/area")}>Reedville</a>. It's fifteen minutes down the road and it earns the drive: Victorian mansions built on menhaden money, the Omega Protein smokestack still running, the Fishermen's Museum open Thursday through Saturday from eleven to four. Stop in. Then lunch at Reedville Market, the kind of spot where you settle in and don't rush.

Back at the cottage by late afternoon. The light on the creek goes gold again, the same as Thursday, and somehow it looks different now that you've been here a couple of days. That's the thing about staying put somewhere good.

## Day Four: Sunday

Sunday morning is the quietest thing the Northern Neck offers. The light comes up gold over the east side of the house, and by the time you've made coffee and found your way to the dock, the ospreys are at it again — diving and rising, working the shallows with that businesslike patience. Watch them long enough and you stop counting the time.

Checkout is eleven. There's a particular quality to that last hour: packing slow, one last walk through the house, standing on the water-side porch before the car gets loaded. The drive back north takes the same two and three-quarter hours. It feels longer, in the best way.
