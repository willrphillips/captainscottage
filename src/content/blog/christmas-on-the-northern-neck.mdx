The first winter we spent out here, I assumed we had bought a summer house. The
creek in July is the obvious version of this place: kids in the shallows, crab
pot coming up at dawn, everybody wet by ten in the morning. December looked like
the off switch.

It is the opposite. The light goes early, the water goes flat and glassy, and
the boats that crowd the channel all summer are up on blocks. What is left is a
house with a sauna, a hot tub and a fire pit, pointed at an empty creek. In
August those are amenities. In December they are the whole reason to be here.

## The house stops being a base and starts being the point

In summer the cottage is somewhere you sleep between things. You are out on the
water, or in Reedville, or driving to a vineyard. The house is a hinge.

Winter inverts it. You get up late because it is dark late. Somebody puts
coffee on. By mid-morning the <a href={withBase("/journal/cottage-sauna-culture")}>sauna</a>
is up to temperature, and because it faces west through a single window, you sit
in the heat and look straight down the creek at water nobody else is using. Then
you walk out to the dock and get in. Thirty seconds, forty if you are showing
off. The cold in December is not the summer cold plunge; it is a different
order of thing entirely, and the ten minutes after it are the best ten minutes
of the trip.

The <a href={withBase("/the-cottage")}>hot tub</a> is on the patio beside the
porch and runs year-round. The fire pit is a few steps from it. We have had
Christmas nights out there with everyone in coats and nobody willing to go in
first.

## What is on, up the road

Kilmarnock is thirty minutes away and does the holidays properly. The town
lights Town Centre Park from November 29 through January 1, five until eight
every night, and you can walk it in twenty minutes with a child who refuses to
wear a hat.

The **46th Annual Lighted Kilmarnock Christmas Parade** runs on **December 11,
2026**. It is a small-town lighted parade, which means fire trucks in tinsel and
a flatbed of carolers, and it is genuinely good.

Two Thursdays in December the town shows a film at the park, free, watched from
your car: **ELF on December 10** and **The Polar Express on December 17**, both
at five. Irvington, forty-five minutes out, runs a holiday marketplace at the
Steamboat Era Museum on weekends from late November through the third week of
December.

That is most of it, and it is enough. You are not coming here for a programme.
You are coming for a quiet house on the water with a parade available if you
want one. More on the towns is in the <a href={withBase("/area")}>area guide</a>.

## The week nobody books

Christmas itself is family logistics. The week after is the actual find.

Between December 26 and New Year's Day the Northern Neck empties out in a way
it does not manage at any other point in the year. The summer crowd is gone, the
hunters are mostly done, and the water sits there. We have walked the beach on
December 28 and seen one other person in two hours.

It is also the week people most need it. You arrive carrying whatever the last
three months did to you, and the house gives you very little to do about it,
which is the treatment. Tide comes in, tide goes out. The heron stands in the
shallows and does not move for an hour. You read more in four days than you
managed all autumn.

New Year's Eve out here is a fire pit and a bottle of something and the water
going completely black by six. No countdown you have to be anywhere for. We
usually miss midnight.

## Coming in winter

Pack for cold and wet feet, because you will be on the dock more than you
expect. Bring whatever you want to cook; the kitchen is better than the local
dinner options on December 27. Everything else is here.

The creek does not close for the season. It just stops being busy, which, if
you have only ever seen this coast in July, is the version worth coming for.
