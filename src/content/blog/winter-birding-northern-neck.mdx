The <a href={withBase("/journal/mornings-with-the-ospreys-hull-creek")}>osprey
that works Hull Creek all summer</a> is gone by October, and the water goes
quiet for a few weeks. Then the ducks show up. Then the geese. Then, some
mornings, something bigger and whiter sits on the far bank that wasn't there
the day before.

Winter on the Northern Neck is a different bird season, not a lesser one.
Northumberland County, the same county as <a href={withBase("/the-cottage")}>Captain's
Cottage</a>, holds two preserves built for exactly this: Hughlett Point and
Dameron Marsh, both a short drive from the cottage on the kind of small
country roads that pass more farms than houses. Both have boardwalks and a
viewing platform, and both are Virginia Department of Conservation and
Recreation land, wintering ground for waterfowl and songbirds and hunting
territory for bald eagles, osprey stragglers, and northern harriers working
the marsh edges low and slow.

## Where the swans actually are

For the swans specifically, the better bet is Belle Isle State Park, about
half an hour past Kilmarnock on the Rappahannock River. Belle Isle carries
seven miles of shoreline and over a hundred documented bird species, and its
calendar flips in the fall the same way Hull Creek's does: osprey work the
park through October, and once they've gone, the tundra swans move in along
with ducks and Canada geese. The swans arrive in November and build through
the winter, biggest numbers in December and January, gone again by spring.
They're unmistakable once you've seen one: five, six feet of wingspan, bright
white, a small yellow mark near the eye if you're close enough to look for it.

Further out, closer to an hour, the Rappahannock River Valley National
Wildlife Refuge adds another few thousand acres of the same idea at a bigger
scale, worth a full morning if the preserves close to the cottage leave you
wanting more.

## A season the locals track on purpose

None of this is a secret to the people who live here. The Northern Neck
Audubon Society runs bird walks most months, posted on nnvas.org, open to
anyone regardless of experience, and every winter they help run a waterfowl
census across the lower Rappahannock and Potomac. The region carries over
250 documented bird species between the two rivers and the Bay, which is the
kind of number that only holds up because enough people keep showing up to
count.
