The <a href={withBase("/journal/mornings-with-the-ospreys-hull-creek")}>osprey
that dives for fish in Hull Creek all summer</a> is gone by October, and the water goes
quiet for a few weeks. Then the ducks show up. Then the geese.

Winter on the Northern Neck is a different bird season, not a lesser one.
Northumberland County holds two preserves built for exactly this: Hughlett
Point and Dameron Marsh, both a short drive from the cottage. Both have
boardwalks, Hughlett Point adds a viewing platform, and both are Virginia
Department of Conservation and Recreation land, wintering ground for waterfowl and
songbirds and hunting territory for bald eagles and northern harriers quartering the
marsh edges low and slow.

## Where the swans actually are

For the swans specifically, the better bet is Belle Isle State Park, a little
past Kilmarnock on the Rappahannock River. Belle Isle carries
seven miles of shoreline and over a hundred documented bird species, and its
calendar flips in the fall too: the last osprey lingers into early October,
and once they've gone, the tundra swans move in along with ducks and Canada
geese. The swans arrive in November and build through
the winter, biggest numbers in December and January, gone again by spring.
They're unmistakable once you've seen one: five, six feet of wingspan, bright
white, a small yellow mark near the eye if you're close enough to look for it.

Further out, closer to an hour, the Rappahannock River Valley National
Wildlife Refuge does the same thing at a much bigger scale, worth a full
morning if the preserves close to the cottage leave you wanting more.

## A season the locals track on purpose

None of this is a secret to the people who live here. The Northern Neck
Audubon Society runs bird walks most months, posted on nnvas.org, open to
anyone regardless of experience, and every winter they help run a waterfowl
census across the lower Rappahannock and Potomac. The region carries over
250 documented bird species between the two rivers and the Bay, which is the
kind of number that only holds up because enough people keep showing up to
count.
