We looked at the cottage in the fall of 2022, and the thing that convinced us
wasn't the view — though the view was everything, west-facing over the creek on
Virginia's Northern Neck, where the Potomac meets the Chesapeake Bay. It was
the feeling that the house already knew what it wanted to be. The structure had
been standing on Hull Creek for decades, and it wasn't trying to be anything
other than what it was: a place to be near the water.

The question every old house asks is which parts are worth saving. Not the
structurally sound parts — that's a different conversation. The parts that have
a feeling, a grain, a personality the place built up over years of other people's
summers. Those are the things you can't get back if you rip them out, and they're
the things that give a house its soul.

Whatever was original to the place and still working, we kept. The instinct to
update everything in a renovation is understandable and usually wrong. Houses
accumulate character the same way people do — slowly, imperfectly, in ways that
can't be replicated in a weekend.

## What we added

The cedar sauna wasn't there when we bought the place. It's the kind of addition
that takes a vacation house from somewhere you sleep to somewhere you return to.

The sauna sits with a west-facing window onto the creek. You heat it until you
mean it, then walk to the end of the dock for the cold of the water. The whole
sequence takes about an hour, and it resets something that two hours in a hammock
can't quite reach.

The hot tub on the deck was added for the same reason, from the same orientation:
facing west, facing the water, in position for the evening light. Everything we
added pointed in the same direction as everything the house already had.
