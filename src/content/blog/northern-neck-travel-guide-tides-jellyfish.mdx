There is a specific afternoon in August I think about when people ask me whether summer is the right time to visit. The creek is almost too warm to be refreshing. The light at seven o'clock turns the water a color that has no name in English — somewhere between bronze and amber — and the jellyfish drift past the dock like slow, translucent kites. They are genuinely beautiful. They can also sting, and they will if you swim into one. That afternoon is the whole truth about summer on the Northern Neck, held in one scene: extraordinary, and worth knowing about in advance.

Most travel guides for this region will tell you when the blue crabs are running and where to find the best soft-shells. None of them will tell you about the jellyfish. That is what this post is for.

## The jellyfish window — and why I tell every guest

The cottage sits on Hull Creek, a tributary off the Potomac River near where it opens into the Chesapeake Bay. We share the water with its wildlife, and in late summer and early fall it is common to see jellyfish — bay nettles, *Chrysaora chesapeakei* — drift past the dock on the current. They can sting swimmers as they pass.

The standard advice, and the one I put in the guest guidebook, is this: **wear skin-covering swimwear**. Swim leggings, a full-body rash guard, and/or a wetsuit. Searching "stinger suit" online turns up inexpensive options that work well. The sting from a bay nettle is uncomfortable, not dangerous to a healthy adult, but it will ruin an afternoon if it catches you off guard. It will not ruin your trip if you came prepared.

Sea nettles are most abundant in brackish water with salinities between 10 and 20 parts per thousand, and they thrive when water temperatures reach the upper 70s to mid-80s Fahrenheit — which describes the Chesapeake in July, August, and into September. Hull Creek runs shallower and fresher than the open Bay, which may mean fewer nettles on any given day, but the host's honest experience is that they do appear at the dock, and the guidebook reflects that.

## Tides: practical, not stressful

Hull Creek runs semidiurnal tides — two highs and two lows per day, a rhythm shared with the Potomac and the Chesapeake. The range here is modest: typically somewhere around two feet on an average day, nowhere near the dramatic draining tides of the Maine coast. What that means practically is that the dock and beach are accessible at most states of tide. You do not need to plan your afternoon swim around a tide window the way you would on an exposed Atlantic beach.

That said, tides do matter for two things: crabbing and kayaking. The crab pots at the dock fish best near slack water. For launching a kayak, a little more water under the hull is always easier.

The nearest NOAA reference station is Point Lookout, MD, Station ID 8578002 — free predictions at tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov. Actual times at Hull Creek will differ slightly from that station.

## Mosquitoes — the screened porches exist for a reason

Waterfront and wooded creek locations in Virginia experience real mosquito pressure from late June through August. Hull Creek is no exception. Afternoon thunderstorms, which roll through regularly in midsummer, create fresh standing water and trigger new waves of breeding; in a heavy-storm week, the evenings can be notable.

## The honest best-months take

June is the month I find myself recommending most often. The water has warmed enough to swim, the jellyfish pressure is lower than it will be in another six weeks, mosquitoes are present but manageable, and crabbing is entering its peak season. The creek lights up gold every evening from May to September, but in June the light lingers longest and the dock is still comfortable well past seven.

Late September and October are the other answer. The jellyfish thin out as water cools, the mosquitoes drop sharply, and the landscape takes on a quality that is hard to photograph but immediately felt — quieter, lower-angle light, fewer boats on the creek. The trade-off is that the water is too cool for swimming unless you are the type who swims in October (some guests are).

July and August are peak summer: warmest water, longest waits for the best weekends, and the full weight of the jellyfish and mosquito seasons. The hot tub and the sauna earn their keep as evening alternatives to open-water swimming. If you come in August, come with a stinger suit and plan on the sauna.
