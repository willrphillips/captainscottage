A lot of DC-area travelers do the Outer Banks once and come back with the same
two complaints: the drive took longer than expected, and the place was more
crowded than they'd imagined. They ask around afterward for alternatives, and
the honest answer that keeps turning up is Virginia's Northern Neck, where the
Potomac River meets the Chesapeake Bay, two hours and forty-five minutes from
DC with no Bay Bridge and no tunnel.

But the drive difference isn't the real story. The Outer Banks and the Northern
Neck are different categories of waterfront weekend, and the comparison between
them isn't a ranking exercise. It's a question about what kind of water you want.

## Two different kinds of water

The Outer Banks is open Atlantic. The waves come in from thousands of miles of
open ocean, the rip currents are real and signposted, the beach stretches wide
and bright and windy. It's a great version of that thing. It delivers on the
surf and the sand and the wide horizon in a way that's hard to argue with.

Hull Creek on Virginia's Northern Neck is a tidal creek fed by the Potomac,
sheltered on both sides, shallow enough in spots to wade across. The water is
warm in summer, calm on most days, and sits about twenty feet from the back
porch at Captain's Cottage. There's no surf, no rip current, no designated
swimming zone. You swim off the end of the dock or wade in from the shore, and
the creek holds you.

These aren't better or worse. They're different responses to the question "I
want to be near the water this weekend."

## The drive and what it means

From DC to the Outer Banks is approximately a five-hour trip on a good day,
and considerably more on a summer Friday with the kind of traffic that turns a
road trip into a logistics problem. From DC to the Northern Neck is two hours
and forty-five minutes: south on 95, east on Route 3 through King George County
and across the Harry W. Nice Bridge into the Neck, and you're pulling onto the
gravel before the afternoon is over.

That two-hour difference compounds across a three-night weekend. A five-hour
drive changes what you can realistically do with a Friday. A two-and-three-
quarter-hour drive lets you leave after work on Thursday and arrive before dark
with energy left over for a drink on the dock.

## The crowd, and what happens without it

The Outer Banks is built to handle millions of summer visitors. The rental
streets, the restaurant rows, the lifeguard stations, the gear shops: there's a
machine that runs the OBX summer vacation and it works efficiently. That's a
feature if you want a built-in program. It becomes a cost if you don't.

The Northern Neck doesn't have that machine. Reedville, the nearest town, has a
Victorian main street, a functioning menhaden plant, and a fishermen's museum
that closes in the afternoon. Kilmarnock has a few restaurants worth finding.
The weekend has no built-in structure. You make it yourself, which tends to mean
you end up with the weekend you actually wanted rather than the one you were
handed.

## Who each one is for

If you want surf, a wide beach, and a vacation destination that has been doing
its job professionally for decades, the Outer Banks does that exceptionally well.
