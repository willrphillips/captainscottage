Before dawn on the Potomac, the workboats are already out. They've been running
these waters on Virginia's Northern Neck, where the Potomac River meets the
Chesapeake Bay, since before anyone thought to call this a vacation destination.
The men who work them are called watermen, and they have their own vocabulary,
their own hours, their own relationship with the tides that doesn't particularly
need to be explained or appreciated by anyone onshore. They're just out there,
the same way they've always been, doing the same work the Chesapeake has
always required.

## What watermen do

A waterman's season follows the water, not the calendar. Blue crabs are the
summer trade: pots set in the dark, hauled at first light, sorted and delivered
before the heat sets in. When the crab season winds down toward October, the
oysters open.

Oyster season on Virginia's tidal reefs opens in early October, and with it the
tong boats and dredge skiffs appear on the creek and the Potomac in numbers you
don't see in summer. Tonging is hand work: two long-handled tongs, a rocking
motion, pulling oysters up from the bottom of the bay. It hasn't changed much
in a hundred years. Private oyster aquaculture has grown on the Northern Neck in
recent decades — cages visible in some of the shallower creeks, farmed oysters
going to restaurants in Richmond and DC — but the public reef harvest is still
done the old way, by men who learned it from their fathers.

The buyboat is the Chesapeake vessel most people outside the region have never
heard of: wide-bottomed, shallow-draft, built to collect catch from smaller
boats and carry it to the processor. They're the boats that made the watermen's
economy work, the link between the creek and the market. You still see them on
the broader Potomac and the Bay, though their numbers have thinned.

## Reedville and the menhaden trade

The visible center of the Northern Neck's working waterfront is Reedville,
fifteen minutes down the road. The Victorian mansions on the main street were
built on menhaden money in the late 1800s, when Reedville was reportedly among
the wealthiest towns per capita in the country. The menhaden plant still runs.
The Reedville Fishermen's Museum, on the waterfront at the end of Main Street,
tells the story of what the fish trade built here and what it cost to build it.
It's worth an hour, and it puts the workboats you've been watching from the dock
into a context that makes them feel different.

## What it means to stay here

Most vacation destinations are adjacent to a history. The Northern Neck is still
inside one. The watermen culture hasn't been memorialized; it's still running,
still waking up before dawn, still following the tides the way it always has.

Staying at Captain's Cottage doesn't make you a participant in that culture. But
it puts you close enough to notice it: the sound of a motor on the creek before
the sun comes up, the sight of a crab pot float bobbing at the edge of a channel,
the particular stillness of the water at slack tide when the whole creek seems to
be holding its breath before the direction changes.
